# Product lens
Score: 6/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **The store answers the buyer's objections directly.** "Will it fit?", "is it see-through or scratchy?", "does the color match the photo?" are all questions that go away once she touches and tries the piece. A fitting room is the product feature the online store cannot offer. That is the best reason for this store to exist.
- **The occasions are emotional and dated.** Eid, Ramadan, weddings and henna nights give the store an experience to design for ("I found my henna-night outfit and it fit"), not just shelves of stock.
- **Real attention already exists.** About 30K TikTok followers and live selling from the store give a first touchpoint the brand already controls.
- **There is care in the details.** Chandeliers, a Turkish-sourced fit-out and planned packaging show the owner thinks about the parts customers see.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **No focus: too many SKUs, channels and jobs at once.** Six marketplaces, a new store, packing online orders in the same space, three new hires, Black Friday four weeks after opening. The overstock (Mira, Lujain, Luna, Marwa, Zina at about 80 to 98 units each) and the dead stock (Noosa, Layla, Wid, Misk) suggest the range was not edited. A customer who walks into a crowded rail of near-identical thobes does not get the "special, not costume" feeling the brand promises.
2. **Seams between online and the store.** One inventory pool shared by a store POS and Shopify plus four marketplaces. If a customer sees a piece on TikTok, drives to Westheimer and it is sold out in her size (or the reverse online), the brand breaks at its most visible point. The same goes for staff on the floor packing parcels while a bride waits for help, and for returns or exchanges between channels with no stated policy.
3. **The experience depends on staff trained in six days, with no defined first five minutes.** Fit advice, fabric explanation and styling with a hijab *are* the product in this store. Nothing in the brief describes the greeting, the fitting-room moment, the try-with-a-hijab moment, or the follow-up after purchase. Unscripted, it will vary from shift to shift.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- In one sentence, why would a Houston woman drive here instead of ordering online?
- What happens in her first five minutes, from the door to the fitting room to the bag? Who owns each step?
- If you could stock only 10 styles on the floor, which ones, and why? What comes off the floor?
- How does store inventory stay in sync with Shopify and the marketplaces, live, on day one?
- What is the true landed cost per piece? The sheet implies about $80 against a $75 to $80 retail price, while the online plan implies about 46% COGS. Which is right?
- Where do online parcels get packed, and when, so they never take the floor staff away from a shopper?
- What is the exchange and return policy across store and online, and is it printed at the till?
- How will you know a store customer came from TikTok (code, question at the till, RSVP match)?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Edit the floor.** Put the top online sellers (Israa, Baydaa, Lujain, Mira and the sets) up front, sized and in depth. Move dead stock to a separate, honestly priced rail or a bundle, not the main display.
2. **Script the first five minutes** (greeting, fit help, try with a hijab, matching-colour check under good light, wrap) and rehearse it before Nov 1.
3. **Connect the POS to one live inventory** with Shopify before opening day, or hold back a store-only allocation per size.
4. **Separate packing** from floor hours or floor staff.
5. **One printed policy** for exchanges and returns across all channels.
6. **Confirm landed cost per piece** before setting the 15% opening discount and Black Friday pricing.
